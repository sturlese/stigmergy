# Alert Fatigue in Cold-Chain Operations

On 30 April 2026, [[Tidewell]] published a blog article on alert fatigue in cold-chain operations, written by [[Joon-ho Park]] and [[Priya Raman]]. (Source: `sources/2026/04/76cb1b34-48f6-50d9-9cc0-199d77e64b7a.md`)

## Argument

The article defines [[Alert Fatigue|alert fatigue]] as the state in which people receive so many alerts that they stop reacting to any of them, and argues it is dangerous in cold-chain operations because the one alert that matters — a compressor failing on a motorway — arrives in the same stream as dozens of harmless door openings. (Source: `sources/2026/04/76cb1b34-48f6-50d9-9cc0-199d77e64b7a.md`)

It reports that Tidewell measured the acknowledged-alert ratio at one large frozen-food customer in December 2025 at 22 percent, against a healthy level above 70 percent. (Source: `sources/2026/04/76cb1b34-48f6-50d9-9cc0-199d77e64b7a.md`)

The article lists four levers that reduce alert fatigue: [[Excursion Budget|excursion budgets]], which alert on cumulative risk instead of every threshold crossing; [[Dwell-Time Awareness|dwell-time limits]], which understand planned stops; grouping alerts by trip, so one journey produces one row; and quiet hours for sites where loading is scheduled. (Source: `sources/2026/04/76cb1b34-48f6-50d9-9cc0-199d77e64b7a.md`)

It concludes that alerting quality, not sensor accuracy, is now the main reason cold-chain monitoring fails in practice. (Source: `sources/2026/04/76cb1b34-48f6-50d9-9cc0-199d77e64b7a.md`)
