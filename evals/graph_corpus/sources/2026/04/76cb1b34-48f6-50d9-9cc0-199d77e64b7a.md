### Readable content

An article written by Joon-ho Park and Priya Raman for the Tidewell blog, 30 April 2026.

Alert fatigue happens when people receive so many alerts that they stop reacting to any of them. In cold-chain operations it is dangerous: the one alert that matters, a compressor failing on a motorway, arrives in the same stream as dozens of harmless door openings.

A useful measure is the acknowledged-alert ratio: the share of alerts that a dispatcher opens and acts on. When Tidewell measured it at one large frozen-food customer in December 2025, it was 22 percent. A healthy operation should be above 70 percent.

Four levers reduce alert fatigue:
- excursion budgets, which alert on cumulative risk instead of every threshold crossing;
- dwell-time limits, which understand planned stops;
- grouping alerts by trip, so one journey produces one row;
- quiet hours for sites where loading is scheduled.

The article argues that alerting quality, not sensor accuracy, is now the main reason cold-chain monitoring fails in practice.
